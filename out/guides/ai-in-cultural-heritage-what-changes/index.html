AI in cultural heritage work: what changes and what does not

By Helen Haritos, Consultant Anthropologist, Connected2Country · Updated 2026-11-05

AI tools can now read a decade of heritage reports in an afternoon, draft a management plan from field notes, transcribe interviews and keep registers current. That changes the economics of desktop work. It does not change what gives heritage work its legal and cultural weight.

What AI does well

  • Finding and summarising what has already been recorded about a place across registers, reports, archives and literature.
  • Drafting documents from the anthropologist's field notes, so that the human time goes into the fieldwork and the judgement.
  • Transcribing and indexing oral histories, with custodian control over access.
  • Keeping consultation registers, condition registers and compliance records current.

What stays with people

  • Identifying who speaks for Country. This is relationship and knowledge, and the answer is contested often enough that it must be defensible.
  • Consultation itself. Consent given to a person on Country is what regulators, courts and communities recognise.
  • Assessing significance and giving expert opinion.
  • Deciding what knowledge is recorded at all, and what must stay restricted.

The risks

  • Confidential cultural information entered into public AI services that retain and reuse inputs.
  • Fluent, confident summaries of sources that do not say what the summary claims. Every AI output in heritage work needs verification against the source.
  • Reports that look complete but were never checked by someone who has been on the Country.
  • Organisations adopting tools with no protocol, so staff make the decisions by accident.

Questions to ask

Who owns the data? Where is it stored and is it used for training? Who verified the output? What is the protocol for restricted knowledge? Would the Traditional Owners whose knowledge this is agree with how it is being handled?

Connected2Country uses AI for the desktop work, verifies everything, and helps organisations write the rules. See AI and digital services for cultural heritage.

General information only, not legal advice. Legislation and agency practice change; check current requirements with AAPA, the Heritage Branch or the relevant land council.
